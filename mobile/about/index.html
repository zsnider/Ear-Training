The Basics

What is ear training?

Ear training is the practice of developing your ability to identify and understand what you're hearing — frequencies, dynamics, space, and texture. Just like sight-reading music or learning an instrument, it's a skill that improves with deliberate, consistent practice.

Experienced mixing engineers don't reach for an analyzer every time they hear a problem — they hear it first. SonicSandbox exists to accelerate that process. Expose yourself to processed audio, make a judgment call, and get immediate feedback. Over time, frequencies start to have colors. Compression starts to have a feel. Space starts to have a shape.

Why It Matters

What better ears actually get you

Faster decisions. When your ears know what to listen for, you spend less time second-guessing and more time making music.

Better mixes. Most mixing problems aren't visible on a spectrum analyzer — they're felt. Trained ears catch problems faster and solve them with fewer moves.

Stronger communication. When you can describe what you're hearing precisely, you communicate more effectively with collaborators and clients.

Translation across systems. Great ears are the ultimate reference monitor.

Audio Sources

Train with any audio — including your own music

Every SonicSandbox game supports four audio source modes. Different sources serve different learning goals.

Pink Noise
Default

A broadband signal with equal energy per octave. The cleanest training signal — nothing to distract you, just the processing itself. Great for learning a concept from scratch.

One Shots

Short transient sounds like claps and snares. Train your ears to catch fast dynamic events — compression artifacts, transient shaping, and frequency coloring all become more obvious on percussive material.

Loops

Real music loops across genres. Training on loops puts your skills in a real-world musical context — you're hearing EQ on a drum groove, not a test signal. This is where fundamentals become instincts.

Your Files
Powerful

Upload a folder of your own music and the games cycle through your tracks automatically. Training on music you've produced is uniquely effective — you already know what it sounds like clean.

Reference

Key terms, explained

New to audio processing? Here are the core concepts that underpin every SonicSandbox game.

EQ (Equalizer)
A tool that adjusts the volume of specific frequency ranges. Boosting 10kHz adds brightness; cutting 300Hz reduces muddiness. EQ is the most fundamental mixing tool.
Frequency
The pitch of a sound, measured in Hz or kHz. Bass lives below 300Hz, mids from 300Hz–4kHz, highs above 4kHz. Learning to identify frequencies by ear is the foundation of mixing.
Compression
Reduces the dynamic range of audio — loud peaks get turned down. The result feels more controlled or glued. Compression shapes transients, sustain, and the overall feel of a mix.
Threshold
The level at which a compressor begins to act. Signals below the threshold pass through unchanged; signals above get compressed. A lower threshold means more of the signal gets processed.
Attack & Release
Attack is how quickly a compressor responds when a signal exceeds the threshold. Release is how quickly it lets go. Fast attack kills transients; slow attack lets them through.
Ratio
How aggressively a compressor reduces gain. A 4:1 ratio means for every 4dB over the threshold, only 1dB comes through. Higher ratios = more squashed.
Reverb
The acoustic echo of a space — sound bouncing off walls and ceilings. Decay time determines how long the tail lasts. Reverb places sounds in a physical environment.
Pre-Delay
A short gap between a dry sound and the start of its reverb tail. Pre-delay preserves clarity while still placing a sound in a space.
Saturation / Distortion
Adding harmonic content by gently (saturation) or aggressively (distortion) clipping a waveform. Tape saturation adds warmth; hard clipping adds grit.
Stereo Width
How spread a sound is across the left-right stereo field. Mono audio sits centered; wide stereo extends to both edges. Controlled by panning, imaging plugins, and mid/side processing.
Q (Resonance / Bandwidth)
In EQ, Q describes how narrow or wide the affected frequency range is. High Q = surgical cut. Low Q = broader, more musical-sounding range.
Dynamic Range
The difference between the quietest and loudest parts of a signal. High dynamic range sounds natural. Compression reduces dynamic range; good mixing balances it.

Best Practices

How to get the most out of ear training

The difference between people who develop great ears and those who plateau is almost always about how they practice, not how much.

01
Short daily sessions beat long occasional ones. Fifteen focused minutes every day beats a two-hour session on weekends. Frequency memory is built through repetition over time.
02
Start with pink noise, then move to music. Pink noise isolates the processing cleanly. Once you know what to listen for, switch to loops — that's where real skill transfer happens.
03
Upload music you've mixed or produced. You already have a mental reference for how your tracks sound dry. Any processing becomes immediately obvious.
04
Always A/B the target before moving on. Don't rush past the feedback — that moment of comparison is where the learning actually happens.
05
Use good headphones or studio monitors. Laptop speakers and earbuds compress dynamic range and distort the stereo image. Any reasonable studio headphones will work.
06
Rest your ears. After 30–45 minutes of critical listening, your accuracy will drop. Take a break and come back fresh.
07
Focus on one game per session when learning something new. Deep reps on one concept beat shallow reps across five.
08
Carry the training into your actual work. Try to identify frequencies by ear before reaching for an analyzer. The games are a gym — your sessions are the real match.
